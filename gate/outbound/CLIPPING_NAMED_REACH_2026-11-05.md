# The hole in the clipping economy (5 Nov 2026)

Not a receipt product. A reach gap next to money that is already being wired for views.

Checked the evening of 5 Nov 2026 (America/New_York). Allegations are labeled as allegations. Ohio is called out because that is where the work would be posted from.

---

## The gap

Buyers are paying real money to manufacture a For You page. The supply they buy is anonymous, often told to hide the payment, and in the best-documented case it is now in court. The scarce object is the opposite account: one named person, on screen, showing the real clip and the real rate, and saying when something is paid.

That account does not exist as a known show. The people in this market are either the buyers, the agencies, or teachers selling a course to other clippers. The audience-facing beat is open. The raw material refreshes every day, which is what makes it able to spread.

## Why the anonymous version is the one in court

NACA v. Polymarket, CEO, and CMO, D.C. Superior Court, filed late June 2026 (2026-CAB-004388). Kelley Drye’s read of the complaint, and the complaint text itself, say the clipping brief told people:

- make it “feel natural and native to the platform”
- “Do NOT make the videos feel like ads or promotions”
- do not put “Polymarket,” or even the prefix “poly,” in the account name (the marketing firm named in the complaint is Virality)
- prove the audience is 60%+ United States

The same complaint alleges simulated betting sites, reshoots, and a class of videos where shown winnings did not match the real market. A follow-on class action, also summarized by Kelley Drye, alleges that across videos in a Wall Street Journal review the creators appeared to win close to $900,000, while the same bets on the real platform would have lost more than $166,000. Allegations. Unresolved. Whop is named as the marketplace and is not the defendant in Kelley Drye’s account of the NACA case.

This is the mess, and it is the point. The cheap CPM exists because the clip is designed not to look like an ad, which is also why ad platforms will not sell the same impression. Forbes’s explanation, via the Bitton interview recap, is that the major ad systems restrict or ban prediction-market promotion, so the buyer goes around them.

## Ohio, specifically

Do not take that bounty, and do not aim prediction-market advertising at Ohio.

CBS Sports, updated 5 Nov 2026: sports contracts are still tradable in Ohio, and prediction markets cannot advertise in the state. The Ohio Casino Control Commission fined Kalshi $5 million; a lower court upheld it; the appeal is live. Google’s prediction-market ad policy has barred those ads in Ohio since 2 June 2026. Polymarket US has sued the state over licensing. Paid clips of these markets are the act the D.C. case and the Ohio ad ban are both about.

Unpaid commentary that quotes a public number is a different act from taking their $1 per thousand views. This note is not a legal opinion that the commentary is in the clear. It is a line between the two.

## What to post, if the name on the screen is his

Three formats, all made from public material, all with his name in the first second. None of them is a Whop submission.

1. **The brief, on screen.** Read the public sentence “Do NOT make the videos feel like ads or promotions,” then show a clip that followed it, then say who got paid and at what rate, citing NPR or the complaint. People share being told they were the target.
2. **The rate card.** One campaign, one number: $1.4 million / 303 people / five weeks, or $25 per thousand versus $0.50 per thousand. The argument in the comments is the distribution.
3. **The warehouse-to-clip path, told once, honestly.** NPR already ran the version with Emrah Bayraktar. The open version is a named person in Toledo showing what a real campaign listing pays, what it asks you to hide, and what he will not post. That is a character, not a farm.

Platform money, if the views are real, is Facebook’s Reels pool and whatever YouTube pays on the same upload. That till is $3 billion and invite-only, so it is not the first dollar. The first dollar is the audience. Buyers who currently want anonymity will not be the sponsor. The sponsors who can attach later are the ones who need a face that already discloses: agencies trying to look clean after this summer’s complaints, and tools that sell to clippers. That is a later sale. The reach is the asset he said he wanted first.

## What this is not

- Not “sign up on Whop and clip Polymarket.” That is the sued workflow, and it hides his name on purpose.
- Not a GEO software company. That market is the opposite of empty: Peec AI said on 2 Nov 2026 it had hit $20 million ARR, and its founder said he had counted 131 rivals. Profound’s September 2026 round was $180 million at a $1.8 billion valuation. No reach, no seat for one person.
- Not a promise that a named account out-earns a farm. The farm is cheaper for the buyer exactly because it is unnamed. The bet is that the unnamed version is the one regulators and viewers are now able to see through, and that a byline is the thing the price list never bought.
