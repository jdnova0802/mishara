# Bucket A — jurisdictional-seam pass (20 Oct 2026)

**Verdict:** no unoccupied seam. The three shapes in the brief were read against GAO, Treasury/FinCEN, the OECD’s 2026 country-by-country peer review, the FATF mutual-evaluation report of Singapore, the IRS country-by-country and private-foundation pages, and House Report 119-768. Each live limit below is one administration’s own sentence. The sentence this brief requires — each of two sovereigns, on its own record, treating the other as the checker, while neither has full jurisdiction — is not in those documents. Do not open a mouth off this pass.

Method: `BUCKET_A_METHOD.md` criterion 4, then object-per-hit, plus the acquisition-check and the “statute already assigns this seat” check. A seam counts only when the document from 2024 forward, tightened to 2025–26 where it exists, names the two regulators, quotes each one’s jurisdictional limit in that regulator’s own words, and shows the structure still operating. A closed case whose seam a later rule already covers does not count. A single regulator that already has the reach is the developmental-statist file (a checker exists, and a stranger cannot re-do the file), and it is killed here. No market-size figure is stated below. The brands are the ones in `gate/family_voices.py`.

| Candidate the brief named | What the 2025–26 record actually assigns |
|---|---|
| Transfer pricing / profit shifting | The ultimate parent files with the tax administration of its residence. That administration exchanges the report. |
| Source-country and destination-country ownership visibility | FinCEN’s domestic exemption, with the 2016 customer-due-diligence rule named as the mitigation. Singapore’s unregistered foreign companies sit with the reporting entity, or with a foreign registry “where available.” |

## Killed: country-by-country reporting — one residence administration files

**OECD (2026), *Country-by-Country Reporting – Compilation of 2026 Peer Review Reports*, Inclusive Framework on BEPS Action 13.** Approved and declassified by the Inclusive Framework on 5 October 2026. https://doi.org/10.1787/353cb719-en

The report’s own description of the design: the ultimate parent entity of a multinational group prepares and files its country-by-country report with the tax administration in its jurisdiction of tax residence. That administration automatically exchanges the report with the tax administration in each jurisdiction listed as a place where the group has a constituent entity, under an international agreement that permits automatic exchange, plus a qualifying competent authority agreement.

Ninth annual peer review, covering 142 jurisdictions. Over 120 have a domestic legal framework. Nineteen received a general recommendation to put in place or finalise that framework. Twenty-five received one or more recommendations on specific areas. One hundred ten have multilateral or bilateral competent authority agreements in place.

**IRS country-by-country FAQ, Treas. Reg. § 1.6038-4 (T.D. 9773).** https://www.irs.gov/businesses/international-businesses/frequently-asked-questions-faqs-country-by-country-reporting The ultimate parent of a U.S. multinational group with $850 million or more of revenue in the relevant preceding period files Form 8975 and Schedules A with its income-tax return. Exchange runs where a legal instrument is in force and a competent-authority arrangement is operative.

FAQ D11, stating the OECD model legislation: foreign subsidiaries of a U.S. group cannot be subject to local filing unless the United States has a legal instrument but has not entered a competent-authority arrangement by the end of twelve months after the fiscal year, or there has been “systemic failure” (a competent-authority arrangement is in place and the United States has suspended automatic exchange for reasons other than the arrangement, or persistently fails to exchange). A foreign jurisdiction that does not meet confidentiality, consistency, and appropriate use cannot require local filing. If the U.S. group is under the U.S. threshold, a foreign jurisdiction cannot require a local subsidiary to file merely because the group exceeds the foreign threshold.

FAQ D9 and D10: certain U.S. limited liability companies, and a U.S. limited partnership or limited liability company that is the ultimate parent and has not elected corporate status, are reported as “stateless.” FAQ D13: a check-the-box election on a foreign eligible entity does not change that entity’s tax jurisdiction of residence for the report. The same FAQ states that the IRS uses the country-by-country data, with other taxpayer data, for high-level assessment of transfer-pricing and other base-erosion risks.

**One line:** the residence jurisdiction is the filer. The other jurisdictions receive the exchange. Local filing is the exception the IRS FAQ walls off. A product that held the intercompany ledger would be selling the file the two administrations do not publish. That is the first answer under criterion 4. The seat the statute and the peer review already name is the ultimate parent’s tax administration.

**Object-per-hit:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who attests | The ultimate parent, on Form 8975 and Schedules A, filed with the tax administration of its residence. |
| What act depends on it | The residence filing, and the automatic exchange that follows where a qualifying competent-authority agreement is in place. |
| Who already gets paid to check | That residence administration. For a U.S. parent, the IRS, under Treas. Reg. § 1.6038-4. The Inclusive Framework peer-reviews whether the framework and the exchange agreements exist. |

**Seat:** assigned. This is the developmental-statist shape: the checker is named, and the underlying intercompany file is not a stranger’s record.

## Killed: beneficial ownership — FinCEN names the bank rule; Singapore names the reporting entity

**FinCEN final rule, effective 14 August 2026.** https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/14/2026-16576/beneficial-ownership-information-reporting-requirement-revision Adopts the 26 March 2025 interim final rule with limited changes. Domestic entities are excluded from the definition of reporting company under 31 U.S.C. § 5336(a)(11)(B)(xxiv). The Secretary, with the written concurrence of the Attorney General and the Secretary of Homeland Security, determined that requiring beneficial-ownership information from domestic reporting companies “would not serve the public interest” and “would not be highly useful” in the listed law-enforcement efforts. The preamble states that exempting domestic entities, and exempting reporting companies from reporting U.S.-person beneficial owners, returns the methods for addressing those risks to the status quo before the reporting rule. It names the continuing obligation of covered financial institutions to conduct customer due diligence under the 2016 customer-due-diligence rule as a method that “significantly mitigates” the illicit-finance risks of domestic entities and of foreign entities with U.S.-person beneficial owners.

A foreign entity formed under foreign law and registered to do business in a U.S. state or tribal jurisdiction by a filing with a secretary of state or similar office remains a reporting company. It does not report beneficial-ownership information of U.S.-person beneficial owners or U.S.-person company applicants, under the exemption FinCEN ties to 31 U.S.C. § 5318(a)(7). A foreign pooled investment vehicle reports a non-U.S. person who exercises substantial control (or the non-U.S. person with the greatest authority). If every such person is a U.S. person, it reports no beneficial owners.

**GAO-26-107967, May 2026.** https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107967 The March 2025 interim final rule’s expanded exemption “applies to over 99 percent of entities that previously were required to report.” States have jurisdiction over formation and reporting inside their borders. Most states collect names of officers, directors, or managers; GAO, citing FinCEN guidance and a 2024 National Association of Secretaries of State report, states that those persons may not be the beneficial owners. The 2016 customer-due-diligence rule collects beneficial-ownership information when an account is opened, not at the legal entity’s creation. GAO’s recommendation is that the Secretary ensure the FinCEN Director identifies potential actions to address the risks of the domestic-company and U.S.-person exemptions and, as appropriate, provides Congress and law enforcement with highly useful information. Treasury disagreed. GAO’s comment summary records FinCEN’s statement that existing measures, including financial institutions’ due-diligence requirements, can help mitigate the risks, and records GAO’s reply that the customer-due-diligence rule does not collect the information at creation and that not all transactions go through covered financial institutions.

**FATF and APG, *Mutual Evaluation Report of Singapore*, May 2026.** https://www.fatf-gafi.org/en/publications/Mutualevaluations/mer-singapore-2026.html Paragraph 83: foreign legal persons (unregistered foreign companies) that are not regarded as carrying on business in Singapore if they only, among other things, maintain a bank account, invest any of their funds, or hold any property, do not need to be registered with the Accounting and Corporate Regulatory Authority. The technical annex, criterion 24.1, cites Companies Act section 366(2) for that list, and adds effecting a sale through an independent contractor. Criterion 24.3: the legal-persons risk assessment rates the money-laundering risk of unregistered foreign companies as high. Paragraph 192: law-enforcement agencies generally share a wide range of information, apart from beneficial-ownership information concerning unregistered foreign companies. Paragraph 383: those companies are not required to register with the Authority or file beneficial-ownership information with the central register. Singapore would access that information by requesting it from the reporting entity where the company is a customer. “In addition, Singapore is also able to seek BO information on Unregistered Foreign Companies from its international counterparts or foreign BO registries (where available).”

**One line:** FinCEN’s own mitigation sentence points at U.S. banks’ customer due diligence. Singapore’s own access sentence points at the reporting entity, and at a foreign registry where one is available. Neither sentence names the other administration as the checker. A foreign company that registers to do business in a U.S. state is inside FinCEN’s reporting rule, which is reach this brief kills as an existing checker. Pairing a U.S. domestic company with a Singapore unregistered foreign company would be an inference across the two documents. The ownership file at the bank, and the file at the reporting entity, are the first answer under criterion 4.

**Object-per-hit:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who attests | For a domestic U.S. company, no beneficial-ownership report to FinCEN. The covered financial institution collects customer-due-diligence information when the legal entity opens an account. For a Singapore unregistered foreign company, the reporting entity holds the beneficial-ownership information. |
| What act depends on it | Opening and keeping the account, or the other activity section 366(2) lists. Formation of a domestic U.S. company does not wait on a FinCEN beneficial-ownership report. |
| Who already gets paid to check | FinCEN, for foreign reporting companies that register in a U.S. state, and for the customer-due-diligence rule it cites. The bank, under that rule. In Singapore, the Accounting and Corporate Regulatory Authority for companies that must register, and the supervisor of the reporting entity for companies that need not. GAO has already written the domestic-exemption gap and assigned the next step to Treasury and FinCEN. |

**Seat:** assigned where a report is due, and unclaimed only as a sentence nobody wrote. GAO’s open recommendation is Treasury’s job. Do not build a mouth that sells either registry’s nonpublic owner file.

## Killed: a foreign grant — the IRS page assigns the U.S. foundation

**IRS, “Grants by private foundations: Expenditure responsibility,” page last reviewed or updated 28 June 2026.** https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/private-foundations/grants-by-private-foundations-expenditure-responsibility Expenditure responsibility means the foundation sees that the grant is spent only for its purpose, obtains full and complete reports from the grantee, and makes full and detailed reports to the IRS. Grants to foreign organizations other than those described in section 509(a)(1), (2), or (3) carry the same restrictions as grants to domestic private foundations. Those restrictions may be phrased under foreign law or custom and ordinarily are sufficient if an affidavit or opinion of counsel states that, under foreign law, the agreement imposes the same restrictions. “Special rules for grants by foreign private foundations. The failure of a foreign private foundation to comply with the restrictions imposed on grants will not constitute an act or failure to act that is a prohibited transaction under section 4948(c) of the Code.”

**House Report 119-768, Stopping Foreign Influence in Elections Act of 2026, 27 August 2026, to accompany H.R. 9771.** https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-119hrpt768/html/CRPT-119hrpt768.htm The Committee on Ways and Means reports that under the Federal Election Campaign Act, foreign nationals are prohibited from donating directly to U.S. campaigns and political committees. “However, there are currently no laws or regulations that prevent situations in which a foreign national makes a contribution to a tax-exempt organization that then subsequently uses such funding to influence elections.” The bill the report accompanies would put excise taxes, and on a third disqualified political contribution a suspension of tax-exempt status, on the tax-exempt organization. The report was committed to the Committee of the Whole on 27 August 2026. It is a committee report on a bill.

**One line:** the IRS page puts the foreign-grant check on the U.S. private foundation and on the report that foundation files with the IRS. Section 4948(c), as the page states it, is a limit on treating a foreign private foundation’s grant failure as a prohibited transaction. The Ways and Means report describes a path with no present U.S. statute and would hand the penalty to the IRS. No destination-country charity regulator’s own jurisdictional sentence was in the corpora read for this pass. The grantee’s spending report is the foundation’s file. Criterion 4 kills a product that sells it.

**Object-per-hit:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who attests | The U.S. private foundation, by the expenditure-responsibility reports it obtains and files. Counsel’s affidavit, where the foundation uses one, states that foreign law matches the domestic restrictions. |
| What act depends on it | The grant’s status as a qualifying distribution rather than a taxable expenditure. |
| Who already gets paid to check | The foundation, which must exercise expenditure responsibility. The IRS, which receives the full report. H.R. 9771, if enacted as the committee describes it, would add a further IRS penalty on a different fact pattern. |

**Seat:** assigned for the private-foundation grant. The committee report’s “no laws” sentence is an absent U.S. rule on political use of a foreign-national gift, with the proposed checker named as the IRS.

## What this pass refuses to call a seam

- A transfer-pricing mouth. The ultimate parent files with its residence administration. One hundred ten jurisdictions have a competent-authority agreement. Local filing of a U.S. group’s report is off unless the IRS FAQ’s competent-authority or systemic-failure conditions are met. The intercompany ledger is not a stranger’s file.
- A paired FinCEN–Singapore ownership mouth. FinCEN’s mitigation sentence is the 2016 customer-due-diligence rule. Singapore’s access sentence is the reporting entity, or a foreign registry where available. GAO-26-107967 already tells Treasury to identify actions on the domestic exemption, and Treasury disagreed. A foreign company registered to do business in a U.S. state remains a FinCEN reporting company.
- A foreign-grant mouth. Expenditure responsibility is the U.S. foundation’s procedure, reported to the IRS. House Report 119-768 would add an IRS penalty. It does not quote a destination charity regulator.
- A pan-regional or remittance-linked fund. No 2025–26 document in the fixed corpora named two regulators’ own jurisdictional limits for a live vehicle of that kind. None is written up.
- The FATF, Egmont Group, INTERPOL, and UNODC handbook, *International Co-operation on Money Laundering Detection, Investigation, and Prosecution* (2025). One hundred six jurisdictions answered questionnaires in September and October 2024. The executive summary records differences in legal frameworks and in the powers available to competent authorities, and a need to foster trust. It names no pair of sovereigns and no live structure whose two regulators each state that the other is already checking.

The seam worth remembering is the missing sentence. Two limits can both be real, current, and written down, and still be two separate limits. This pass had those limits. It did not have each sovereign saying the other one was the checker.
