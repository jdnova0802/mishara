# Bucket A — public-choice / regulatory-capture pass (20 Oct 2026)

**Verdict:** no unoccupied seat. The capture shape is real in the files below. In each one, a named body already attests, the license or the payment already depends on that attestation, and a statute or a contract already names who must check it. Where the check is weak, an inspector general or GAO has already written the weakness down and assigned the repair to that same body. A file that only the firm and the agency hold is the first answer under criterion 4. Do not open a mouth off this pass.

Method: `BUCKET_A_METHOD.md` criterion 4, then object-per-hit, plus the acquisition-check and the “statute already assigns this seat” check. This pass adds the capture screen the brief asked for: the dependency has to be in a GAO report, an OIG report, a consent instrument, the Federal Register, or a current statute, from 2024 forward, tightened to 2025–26 where the document exists. No candidate below comes from a think piece. LegiScan did not supply a bill that reached a write-up. A hit counts only when the checker’s financial, career, or structural dependence on the attested party is still unverified by anyone a stranger can use, the confirming seat is empty or the only attestation is the firm’s, and an inspector general or GAO does not already cover that confirmation. Captured-and-already-fixed is a kill. The brands are the ones already in `gate/family_voices.py`. No new brand. No market-size figure is stated below.

## Killed: DOE Loan Programs Office — the advisor on both sides of the loan

**DOE-OIG-25-32, issued 26 August 2025.** https://www.oversight.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/2025-08/DOE-OIG-25-32.pdf The Loan Programs Office uses contractors and third-party advisors to review applications, assess projects, and support loan decisions. The report states that some of those advisors are also used by loan applicants, and that there are a limited number of firms with the experience. During the review the office used over 300 contractor and subcontractor employees, more than six times the September 2020 count.

What the audit found, in the report’s own counts:

- For 18 projects that had a conditional commitment or had reached financial close from January 2021 through May 2024, the office could identify engineering third-party advisors for 3 projects and could not identify marketing or financial third-party advisors for any.
- Of 25 approved conflict-of-interest waiver requests reviewed, 22 were approved on the firm’s self-certification that it was complying with its mitigation plan. The contracts did not require the firm to give the office the plan. The office could not produce the plans until it asked the firms. For 2 of the 25, the office could not produce a justification.
- The prime contractor, Archetype, submitted an organizational-conflict plan in October 2017. As of the audit it had not implemented the plan’s training program, had not submitted the annual certifications, and had no records of audits of subcontractors. One subcontractor with no such training, and none from Archetype, employed over half of the employees doing the office’s work in the period reviewed. The contracting officer’s representative did not obtain a copy of the plan until July 2024.

The office had told a 2012 OIG inquiry (OAS-RA-12-14) that it had a tracking system for law-firm waiver requests. The 2025 audit found the disclosures were still compiled from email and from a law firm’s folder on a shared drive.

FAR 9.504 and 9.506 assign identification of organizational conflicts to the contracting officer. DEAR 952.209-72 assigns disclosure and mitigation to the contractor and the waiver to the contracting officer. Management concurred with both recommendations: a centralized tracking system for every party on a project and for every disclosure and waiver, and procedures to monitor Archetype’s plan. The OIG called those proposed actions responsive. The report does not record that the repository was already operating.

**One line:** this is the capture shape. The person paid to help the government decide is also available to the borrower, and the office accepted the firm’s word that the mitigation plan was being followed. The repair the OIG accepted is an internal Department system. A product that published which advisor sits on both sides would be selling the nonpublic list. That is the first answer under criterion 4. A receipt that merely says “a determination exists” is the contracting officer’s existing duty.

**Object-per-hit:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who attests | The contractor and the third-party advisor, by the self-certification and the disclosure. On 22 of 25 waivers reviewed, that self-certification was the justification. |
| What act depends on it | The loan and the loan guarantee. The report’s scope is projects that had a conditional commitment or had reached financial close. |
| Who already gets paid to check | The contracting officer, under FAR 9.504 and DEAR 952.209-72. The Inspector General has already audited the failure and accepted an internal fix. |

**Seat:** assigned. Do not build an Erra gate, or a Gate halt, in front of an LPO close. The brands map as follows: Erra asks whether to close; Gate asks whether the disbursement write completes; Velaru would be a stranger’s copy of the waiver file. All three die on the same seat.

## Killed: FDA 510(k) third-party review — the sponsor pays the reviewer

**FDA page, read 20 October 2026.** https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/510k-third-party-review-program The page’s update line is 20 November 2024, for the final guidance “510(k) Third Party Review Program and Third Party Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) Review.” The page states: “The sole payment under the program is between the 510(k) submitter and the 3P510k Review Organization; there is no separate payment (i.e., user fee) to the FDA.” The organization recommends substantially equivalent or not substantially equivalent. “The FDA makes the final determination.” The same page says the agency “is updating this program to avoid the routine re-review of 510(k) submissions already reviewed by a 3P510k Review Organization,” and that the agency may still re-review if the organization misapplied the criteria or the documentation has substantive quality problems. The decision clock on the page is 30 days after the recommendation.

**GAO-26-108499, published 21 September 2026.** https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108499 From fiscal years 2018 through 2025, third parties sent FDA 617 reviews and recommendations, about 2 percent of the Center for Devices and Radiological Health’s 510(k) reviews a year. FDA told GAO it had accredited 32 third parties since the program began; as of May 2026, nine had active accreditations. FDA conducted 25 periodic audits from 2000 to 2026: 13 from 2000 through 2003, five from 2011 through 2013, two in 2022, and five from 2025 to 2026. GAO found the audit policies omit target time frames for finishing an audit and telling the organization the result. Several recent audits with deficiencies, including standard-operating-procedure language GAO describes as too vague, took more than six months to close. GAO made one recommendation: update the audit policy to include the existing selection criteria, current post-audit practice, and those time frames. HHS concurred. The recommendation is open.

**One line:** the sponsor pays the reviewer, and FDA’s own page says routine re-review is what the program is moving away from. The marketing decision is still FDA’s, under the program Congress assigned in the 1997 statute GAO cites. GAO’s open item is FDA’s audit clock, which FDA agreed to write. A product that re-did the 510(k) would be selling the submission. Mishara’s harm question does not open a second seat on that file: the clearance is public, the audit findings GAO reviewed are the agency’s.

**Object-per-hit:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who attests | The accredited third party, in a recommendation the submitter paid for. |
| What act depends on it | FDA’s substantial-equivalence determination, which the page says remains FDA’s. |
| Who already gets paid to check | FDA, on the final determination and on the periodic audit. GAO has already said the audit policy is missing a clock. |

**Seat:** assigned. Do not build a Velaru receipt, or a Mishara record, in front of a 510(k) clearance.

## Killed: Coast Guard third-party vessel certification

**GAO-25-107139, published 4 February 2025.** https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-107139 Statute and regulation let the Coast Guard authorize third-party organizations to perform inspection and certification work. Owners and managing operators may elect those options. In 2023 the organizations helped support certification of 22 percent of the U.S.-flagged commercial vessels that required inspection. Use grew from 483 vessels in 2018 to 3,786 in 2023. Under the Towing Safety Management System option, a third party verifies the owner’s system and can issue that certificate; the owner may then obtain a Coast Guard certificate of inspection without an annual Coast Guard inspection. From 2018 through 2023 the Coast Guard conducted 12 vertical contract audits, covering six of the 14 organizations authorized in 2023.

The Coast Guard had not, as of the report, built the performance-measurement system its 2018 guidance called for, and it had not reassessed the benefits and risks of using these organizations since 2017. Recommendation 1, on recording towing-vessel audit observations, is closed: the Coast Guard updated two procedures in September 2025 and gave them to GAO in May 2026. Recommendations 2, 3, and 4 were open as of May and June 2026. The service’s own dates, as GAO records them, are 31 December 2026 for the performance system and the benefits-and-risks plan, and 31 December 2027 for putting the recording fix into the new case-management system.

**The fee rule that states the contract.** https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/12/28/2023-28112/user-fees-for-inspected-towing-vessels The rule is 28 December 2023, before this pass’s time box. It is cited only for the structural sentence the 2025 report does not spell out: “A vessel owner or operator who selects the TSMS option is making a business decision that should account for the cost to contract with a third-party organization.” The rule’s annual Coast Guard user fees, which are the government’s fee and not the third party’s fee, are $973 for the subchapter M TSMS option and $2,184 for the Coast Guard option, and $1,850 for the subchapter I Alternate Compliance Program option and $2,747 for the Coast Guard option. The rule says about 70 percent of subchapter M certificates of inspection were on the TSMS option.

**One line:** the owner elects the organization, the organization attests, and the Coast Guard still issues the certificate of inspection. The open GAO items are the Coast Guard’s data and its performance system. A stranger’s copy of the survey would be the vessel file. That is the first answer under criterion 4.

**Object-per-hit:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who attests | The third-party organization, in the survey, audit, or Towing Safety Management System certificate the owner uses. |
| What act depends on it | The Coast Guard certificate of inspection. On the TSMS option, that certificate can issue without an annual Coast Guard inspection. |
| Who already gets paid to check | The Coast Guard, which authorizes the organization and issues the certificate. GAO’s open recommendations tell the Coast Guard to finish its own measurement system. |

**Seat:** assigned. 46 U.S.C. § 3316 is the authority GAO cites. Do not build a Velaru inspection receipt, or a Gate halt, on a certificate of inspection.

## Killed: Boeing ODA — employees of the firm perform the FAA act

**DOT OIG AV2025002, issued 9 October 2024.** https://www.oig.dot.gov/library-item/46466 The Boeing Organization Designation Authorization is Boeing employees authorized to perform tasks FAA delegates, including inspections and airworthiness certificates. The report found FAA had not established criteria for returning delegated authority to that unit. It also found FAA had not enforced the requirement that Boeing report undue-pressure allegations in enough detail.

**OIG recommendation dashboard, read 20 October 2026.** https://www.oig.dot.gov/recommendation-dashboard The AV2025002 line says 10 of 16 recommendations are closed. Closed on 24 September 2025: establish specific and measurable criteria before FAA returns delegation to Boeing’s ODA, and enforce the undue-pressure reporting requirement. Still open: recommendations 3, 5, 6, 7, 9, and 11. Those six are FAA’s audit-system functionality, supplier-audit guidance, disposition of joint-review recommendations, and inspector training.

**FAA news release, 17 July 2026.** https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/after-months-safety-review-faa-allows-boeing-resume-issuing-certificates-new-airplanes Effective 20 July 2026, FAA allows Boeing to issue airworthiness certificates for all 737 MAX and 787 airplanes. The release says FAA had Boeing and FAA issuing those certificates on alternating weeks beginning September 2025, saw comparable production-quality findings over eight months, and returned the task. It says FAA renewed Boeing’s ODA for three years in May 2025, and that FAA will keep inspecting and auditing production. The unit members remain an organization inside the company.

**Department of Justice non-prosecution agreement, 29 May 2025.** Boeing’s 8-K: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/12927/000001292725000041/ba-20250529.htm The agreement: https://www.justice.gov/criminal/media/1402426/dl?inline= It resolves the Offices’ determination that Boeing breached the 7 January 2021 deferred-prosecution agreement. The criminal penalty is $487,200,000, of which $243,600,000 was paid under the deferred-prosecution agreement. The agreement also requires $444.5 million for heirs and beneficiaries of the Lion Air Flight 610 and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 deaths, at least $455 million invested in compliance, safety, and quality programs, and an Independent Compliance Consultant retained by the company. The company and the consultant report to the Offices during the term. The statement of facts is the 737 MAX differences-training fraud on FAA’s Aircraft Evaluation Group, not a finding about the 2026 certificate return. The consultant’s reports go to the Offices. They are not a stranger’s check of each airworthiness certificate.

**One line:** the career dependency is structural. The people who perform the delegated FAA act are Boeing’s employees. The 2024 finding that FAA had no criteria for giving that act back is closed, and FAA then gave the certificate pen back under a comparison it ran itself. The criminal agreement puts a company-retained consultant in front of the Offices for the fraud-compliance program. It does not put a stranger in front of the certificate. The open OIG items are FAA’s own systems.

**Object-per-hit:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who attests | Boeing’s ODA unit members, who are Boeing employees performing delegated FAA tasks. |
| What act depends on it | The airworthiness certificate. From 20 July 2026, Boeing may issue it again for the 737 MAX and the 787. |
| Who already gets paid to check | FAA, which delegates under 14 C.F.R. Part 183 Subpart D, sets the return criteria the OIG recommendation required, and says it continues to inspect. The Justice Department’s consultant reports to the Offices on the compliance program. |

**Seat:** assigned. Do not build a Gate halt, or a Mishara receipt, on the certificate. A product that used nonpublic production data to second-guess it would fail criterion 4.

## Killed: Medicare Advantage diagnosis codes — the plan submits the record CMS pays on

**HHS OIG A-02-23-01020, May 2026.** https://www.oig.hhs.gov/documents/audit/11676/A-02-23-01020.pdf CMS pays Medicare Advantage organizations more when submitted diagnosis codes map to a higher-cost condition. The report states that CMS relies on the organizations to collect the codes from providers and submit them. For all 97 sampled enrollees, the acute-stroke codes were not validated: for 93 the medical records did not support the codes, and for 4 the organizations could not locate the records. The findings section estimates $461,958,186 in potential net overpayments for 2021. The highlights round that estimate to $462 million. The report also states that for 2024 CMS paid approximately 760 organizations $494 billion. That figure is the report’s description of the program, not a market size.

OIG recommended one procedure: stop the overpayment when an acute-stroke code arrives on a physician record and the enrollee has no acute-stroke diagnosis on a hospital record in the same service year. CMS did not specify concurrence or nonconcurrence. The payment authority the report cites is Social Security Act §§ 1853(a)(1)(C) and (a)(3) and 42 C.F.R. § 422.308.

**One line:** the plan’s submission is the data the payment uses, and the audit says CMS’s procedures did not keep these unsupported codes out. The repair is a CMS procedure. The chart is the nonpublic file. OIG has already sampled it.

**Object-per-hit:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who attests | The Medicare Advantage organization, by the diagnosis codes it submits. |
| What act depends on it | The monthly risk-adjusted payment. |
| Who already gets paid to check | CMS, which runs the risk-adjustment program. The Inspector General has already tested the high-risk codes and recommended a CMS screen. |

**Seat:** assigned. Do not build a Velaru chart-check, or a Mishara record, in front of the payment. Selling the medical record is the first answer under criterion 4.
