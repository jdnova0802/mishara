# Attention-economy leads — three checks (5 Nov 2026)

Not a kill-for-not-looking-like-Aave pass. Each lead is scored on the actual wedge: a missing fact a stranger can check, a named dispute, a dollar figure from a source, and a party positioned to pay for the receipt.

Checked the evening of 5 Nov 2026 (America/New_York). Sources are linked inline. Where a number is a pleading, a settlement stipulation, or a television estimate, it is labeled as that.

---

## 1. Brand–influencer deliverable disputes — partial, and the dollars that reached a decision are small

### Who actually filed, and for how much

**PR Consulting, Inc. v. Luka Sabbat** (client: Snap Inc. / Spectacles). Contract dated 15 Sep 2018. Sabbat was to make one Instagram feed post and three Stories, with pre-approval, analytics, swipe-up links, and public photographs in Spectacles at Milan and Paris Fashion Weeks. Pay was $45,000 up front plus $15,000 later ($60,000). PR Consulting alleged he delivered one feed post and one Story, skipped the rest, and refused to return the advance. Suit filed in New York Supreme Court; Kelley Drye (1 Nov 2018) says the complaint sought the $45,000 plus interest, fees, and other damages, and that Sabbat allegedly admitted default. Settled August 2019: Judge Arthur Engoron entered a stipulation that Sabbat pay $15,000 within 10 days and produce a confession of judgment. The Fashion Law (6 Aug 2019) reads that as Sabbat keeping $30,000 for the one feed post he did make.

Sources: [Kelley Drye, 1 Nov 2018](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/company-sues-influencer-for-failing-to-influence), [Kelley Drye, settlement note](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/when-influencers-fail-to-influence), [The Fashion Law, 6 Aug 2019](https://www.thefashionlaw.com/luka-sabbat-and-pr-consulting-settle-suit-over-influencers-failure-to-influence/), [TechCrunch, 31 Oct 2018](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/31/influencer-marketing-lawsuit/).

**Catwalk to Sidewalk Inc. (Konus) v. Luka Sabbat and Clark Sabbat**, Los Angeles Superior Court, filed 2019. Konus says it paid $30,090 for a Fall/Winter 2017 lookbook shoot plus two Instagram images, that he did the shoot and did not post the images, and that it sought $40,000. No judgment or settlement turned up in the sources checked.

Source: [Variety, 2019](https://variety.com/2019/biz/news/instagram-influencer-luka-sabbat-bilking-brand-1203258047/).

**Chloe Roberts v. Legacy Camberwell (Con Katsogiannis)**, Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal, Deputy President Ian Lulham, decided about 6–9 May 2019. Oral deal, $200 then $300 per post. Invoice in dispute about A$2,100 (a meeting figure of A$2,250 is also reported). Owner could not find the posts on her feed and said she was billing for nothing. She said she had archived them: still retrievable, invisible on the grid, and that about 90% of views happen in the first week. No written term on how long a post had to stay visible. Lulham likened the service to a billboard, found the owner had not lost value “in a general sense,” and said he could not decide whether she was allowed to archive at will. Order: two-thirds of the sum sought, **A$1,400, plus A$276 filing fees (A$1,676)**. K&L Gates’ writeup of the same reasons says the tribunal implied a “reasonable number of posts,” creative control, and no fixed duration, and told brands to write a minimum visibility period into the contract.

Sources: [ABC, 10 May 2019](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2019-05-10/instagram-influencer-wins-pay-dispute-with-cafe-owner/11099882), [SmartCompany](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/business-advice/chloe-roberts-influencer-legacy-cafe/), [K&L Gates, “Social sense” (PDF)](https://files.klgates.com/webfiles/casinader_Social_Sense.PDF).

**No:ze (South Korea, July 2022).** Not a judgment. After Street Woman Fighter, she was accused of deleting sponsored posts for smaller brands and keeping luxury ones. Her agency then said it had failed to keep contract periods it had promised. A lawyer on KBS entertainment coverage said a penalty could run to twice the model fee, and the segment assumed 30–50 million won per post, which the same coverage turned into “tens to hundreds of millions of won.” That is a television estimate, not a filed claim with a result.

Source: [KBizoom summary of the KBS segment, 15 Jul 2022](https://kbizoom.com/sns-advertisement-power-abuse-controversy-noze-may-be-sued-for-hundreds-of-millions-of-won/).

### What is not this lead

NAD activity on Kelley Drye’s blog is disclosure, not “you never posted.” Mielle Organics v. Kreyol Essence (Laura Benoit, affiliate posts, Oct 2024 emails, NAD went ahead because takedown finished after the complaint) and the Fenty Skin inquiry (Ms. Fatale, Sarah Novio) are about whether a material connection was clear. NAD is an advertising-truth forum. It does not decide a contract’s deliverable count.

The [terms.law demand-letter page](https://terms.law/Demand-Letters/Influencer-Media/brand-influencer-contract-breach-demand-letters.html) is a template shop. No client name, no letter that was sent, no dollar that changed hands. It is not evidence that firms are issuing these letters at any volume.

Gymshark v. Alix Earle (High Court, settled by March 2025; she alleged a $1 million package was pulled over a political Instagram post) is a morals / termination fight. A receipt that the post existed would not have been the missing fact. Cited only so the large number is not borrowed for the deliverable-uptime lead.

### Would a signed “up at X, stayed for Y” receipt have resolved a named dispute?

**Roberts: it would have cleaned the fact the fight started on, and it would not have decided the case.** The owner’s claim was “the posts are not there, so this invoice is for nothing.” A stranger’s log of publish time and archive time is exactly that fact. Lulham still had to price the service without a duration clause, and he split the invoice because he could not say she was forbidden to archive. The receipt removes the proof fight. The legal leftover — was archiving allowed, and what was the post worth after the first week — is a contract term, not a missing observation. The whole judgment is A$1,676. A retainer in the range already scoped for the webhook offer is larger than the case.

**Sabbat: the receipt was not the bottleneck.** PR Consulting’s complaint already specified which posts existed (one feed, one story) and which did not. Stories expire in 24 hours, so a watcher is the only durable record of a Story if the creator denies it. Here Kelley Drye reports he allegedly admitted default. The items a public-web receipt cannot see were a large part of the breach as pleaded: pre-approval, analytics sent to the firm, and being photographed in public at two fashion weeks. Settlement math was about money already paid ($15,000 back of $45,000), which is the point Kelley Drye actually draws: do not pay the whole fee before the milestones. A receipt does not collect a judgment.

**Konus: a receipt would have been the whole pleaded fact** (two images never posted), on a $30,090 payment and a $40,000 demand. Disposition unknown. Same collection problem as Sabbat if he had the money and would not return it.

### Who would pay

The brand-side “is it still live” check is already a feature inside campaign tools and contract forms, not an empty seat:

- Influenxio’s creator MSA defines a Live Period, makes the fee contingent on the deliverable staying public for that whole period, and pays net 30 after the period ends. [Influenxio MSA](https://influenxio.ai/services-agreement/).
- nowfluence Percept and HypeAuditor sell continuous campaign watching, including Stories captured before they expire. Those are the brand’s own logs. They are not a stranger both sides accept when the brand is the one the creator does not trust.

A stranger-signed receipt earns its fee only when the brand’s screenshot is the disputed exhibit. That happened in Roberts because there was no written contract and no monitor, over a cafe invoice. It did not happen in Sabbat, where the agency could already say which posts ran. No public source shows a brand, agency, or creator paying a third party, as opposed to its own platform or its own lawyer, to certify uptime.

**Call:** real disputes, real names, one tribunal outcome where the missing observation is post-existence-and-duration. The decided case is four figures. The five-figure case settled on an admitted miss plus money already wired, and half the pleaded breach is off-platform. NAD is the wrong docket. This is a narrow fact with a price ceiling, not an empty category and not a mouth.

## 2. Esports anti-cheat bans — the pain has a price; nobody in a position to pay can see the fact

### Olkkonen v. Valve GmbH

Elias “Jamppi” Olkkonen sued in the District Court of Eastern Uusimaa (Itä-Uudenmaan käräjäoikeus), claim filed around February 2020. He wanted a VAC-linked Major ban lifted and **€268,092** for a lost OG offer. The account was created in 2015, when he was 14; his case is that he sold it the same night to a friend at a LAN, and the cheat happened later. He sued **Valve GmbH**, a German subsidiary founded in 2016, not Valve Corporation.

Valve GmbH’s response, reported by Ilta-Sanomat and Dexerto: wrong company, wrong grounds. GmbH manufactures devices, does not own or run Counter-Strike, did not exist in 2015, and the contract from the 2015 account creation was with Valve Corporation in the United States. It also said the damages claim cannot run through a third party’s decision not to hire him, and asked the court to refuse the case before more costs piled up.

The district court, decision 10 Nov 2020, left the claim unexamined. Reasons that are in Yle’s account of the judgment: Olkkonen was a professional, not a consumer, so the Finnish court had no jurisdiction against a German defendant, and he had no contract with Valve GmbH. He was ordered to pay Valve GmbH’s costs of **€44,650**, on top of his own **€33,854**. Counsel was Hannu Kalkas of Teperi & Co.

The Helsinki Court of Appeal left that standing and cut the costs: Valve GmbH’s district-court costs down to **€20,000**, plus **€9,540** for the appeal. Further appeal was open until 28 Dec 2021. The merits — who controlled the account when the cheat fired — were never tried.

In spring 2021 Valve lifted the ban as part of a broader rule change, after Olkkonen had moved to Valorant. Richard Lewis, interviewed by Ilta-Sanomat, called the handling a misuse of publisher power and said the ban was cleared only once the Counter-Strike career was already gone. He also said ESIC had failed to fill the gap except in a few titles.

The “weak case” label in the press is this procedural defect, and it is Valve’s own characterization (“wrong company”), confirmed by two courts. It is not a finding that the ban was factually right. Separately, Valve’s Finnish lawyers told Dexerto that the account data does not show anyone but Olkkonen in control when the cheat happened, and that a Finnish report claiming both sides had agreed he was not in control was wrong. His lawyers told Dexerto the sale itself was not disputed on the pleadings. Those are competing readings of the papers. No court picked one.

Sources: [Yle](https://yle.fi/a/3-11644256), [Ilta-Sanomat, Valve’s response](https://www.is.fi/esports/art-2000006542939.html), [Ilta-Sanomat, appeal](https://www.is.fi/esports/art-2000008372532.html), [Dexerto, wrong company](https://www.dexerto.com/csgo/csgo-pro-jamppi-sued-wrong-company-over-valve-vac-ban-1380125/), [Dexerto, control dispute](https://www.dexerto.com/csgo/valve-dispute-claims-made-in-finnish-reports-relating-to-jamppi-lawsuit-1479994/), [Ilta-Sanomat, Richard Lewis](https://www.is.fi/esports/art-2000011176561.html).

### The wider pattern, and who has actually paid

**Riot / Renegades, May 2016.** Riot permanently barred Renegades (and sanctioned Team Impulse and Team Dragon Knights) from the LCS. ESPN reported Riot would not release the evidence, citing protection of sources, and that the ban potentially cost the team and its owners future revenue and sponsorships. No independent reviewer was hired. The publisher held the file and declined to open it. [ESPN](https://www.espn.com/gaming/story/_/id/17132668/renegades-riot-danger-absolute-power).

**PaiN Gaming v. Riot Brazil**, mid-2010s. The club sued after a poaching sanction it said was imposed without the proof it had asked for. The Esports Observer reported an urgent application was denied and the suit continued. Again the demand was “Riot, show your file,” not “hire a stranger to reconstruct it.” [Esports Observer archive](https://archive.esportsobserver.com/pains-lawsuit-riot-just-beginning/).

**FACEIT / LOBOARMY, Enmanuel “SJR” Villar.** The org’s public ask, reported by Dust2.us, was a letter to ESIC and tickets to FACEIT requesting the cheat type and the match. FACEIT’s side told Dust2 the wave was not false positives. FACEIT’s own policy is an internal support appeal; cheating bans stay if prohibited software ran, including software a different person ran on that PC. [Dust2.us](https://www.dust2.us/news/61967/loboarmy-maintains-sjrs-innocence-despite-faceit-ban), [FACEIT](https://support.faceit.com/hc/en-us/articles/360015762780-My-ban-is-not-justified-what-can-I-do).

**IGET** is a real appeal seat, built with WIPO, and it replaced ESIC’s old appeals panel. It hears ESIC decisions, de novo, on a fast written track. The appealing party can be ordered to bear the costs if the appeal is frivolous or in bad faith. It does not sit over Valve VAC or Riot Vanguard. [IGET appeals](https://www.iget.gg/esic-appeals-process.html), [ESIC](https://esic.gg/international-games-and-esports-tribunal/).

### Buyer test

A player will pay a lawyer. Olkkonen did, and the spend that is on the record is tens of thousands of euros in costs against a €268,092 claim that was never heard. An org will write to the league and ask for the log. Nobody in these sources paid a third party to certify the ban, because the contested fact is telemetry on the publisher’s machines: process lists, account-control history, detection signatures. A stranger watching the public match cannot sign “no cheat was running,” and a receipt the player signs himself is the exhibit the publisher already ignores. The party that has the fact treats disclosure as a loss (cheat signatures, confidential sources). That party is the adversary, not the customer.

**Call:** not empty pain. Priced pain, a lawsuit that failed for a reason the defendant’s own filing predicted, and a later ban lift that came from the publisher changing a rule rather than from a proof product. No buyer who can both pay and produce the fact. This is not a mouth.

## 3. YouTube / FTC — the read is right; kill the wedge, keep the probe as a fact

The official record is the FTC’s 20 Feb 2025 Request for Information on technology-platform censorship, docket **FTC-2025-0023**, comments closed 21 May 2025. Regulations.gov shows **2.89K** comments. The RFI asks whether platforms deny or degrade access (shadow bans, demonetization, suspensions) in ways that contradict their policies or public representations, and whether that is an unfair or deceptive act under the FTC Act or an unfair method of competition. It asks how users were notified and whether they could appeal. It does not ask a stranger to decide whether a given video violated a rule.

[FTC press release, 20 Feb 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/federal-trade-commission-launches-inquiry-tech-censorship), [RFI PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P251203CensorshipRFI.pdf), [Regulations.gov docket](https://www.regulations.gov/docket/FTC-2025-0023).

Bloomberg reported on 27 Aug 2026, via people familiar with a confidential probe, that the FTC had been looking at YouTube since 2025 and was in the late stages of a possible complaint: users may have been led to sign up believing certain content was allowed, then had videos removed or accounts suspended. YouTube had not been accused. The agency’s spokesman declined to comment. Investigations of this kind end without a complaint often enough that the reporting says so. Secondary writeups (Business Standard, Outlook) add that the Bureau of Consumer Protection is the shop, and name bureau director Chris Mufarrige; that detail is attribution to Bloomberg, not an FTC docket entry. No complaint was found on ftc.gov.

A signed receipt that “this video was public at time T” or “this channel was suspended at time T” is a fact the creator already has from the strike email and the upload log. It does not answer the FTC’s question, which is whether YouTube’s published rules matched the standard it actually applied. That comparison needs YouTube’s internal enforcement record. The only party positioned to compel it is the Commission. Creators in the 2.89K comments are complainants, not customers of a proof product. A product that certified individual uptime would not be evidence in that case, and a product that opined “this suspension matched the guideline” would be a legal conclusion, which is outside the wedge on purpose.

**Call:** kill. The probe is real. The missing fact is a deception theory about the platform’s own rules, held by the platform, pursuable by a regulator. It is not a stranger-verifiable event a private party would pay Nisaba to sign.
