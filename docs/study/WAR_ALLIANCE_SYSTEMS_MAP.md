# War & Alliance Systems Map

Descriptive / institutional reference. **Who holds the decision, and what obligates anyone to act.** Not a history of wars and not a casualty ledger. Primary sources first. Wikipedia is a finding aid. OPEN rather than invented. Pull date **2026-11-14**. Companion to the Atlas, Religion as Institution Map, and Standards & Certification Map — **not** the same field grid. Standing rules for this series: [`docs/study/STUDY_SERIES_RULES.md`](STUDY_SERIES_RULES.md).

Do not mash machines. A Chapter VII Security Council resolution is not Article 51 self-defence. **NATO** (North Atlantic Treaty Organization) Article 5 is not an automatic war order. An Authorization for Use of Military Force is not a declaration of war. A presidential covert-action finding is not a treaty. **IEEPA** (International Emergency Economic Powers Act) sanctions are not Chapter VII unless a resolution so decides.

## How to read an entry

Every entry uses the same fixed **12-field** template. Field labels stay in this form throughout. In any **Falsifier** line, “cell N” means field N on this list (so “cell 6” is Obligation type). **Automatic** means the text itself requires a specified military response without a further political choice of *whether* to act. **Consultative / discretionary** means the text requires consultation, reporting, or “such action as it deems necessary,” leaving the *whether* and *how* to a named constitutional actor.

1. **Mechanism/entity name**
2. **Type** — legal declaration mechanism / alliance treaty / deterrence doctrine / proxy-conflict pattern / hybrid-warfare instrument
3. **Legal basis** — treaty article, statute, or constitutional clause, cited and (where short) quoted
4. **Decision authority** — the specific office or body that holds the trigger (never “the government” or “the United States (US)”)
5. **Trigger condition** — the mechanism’s own language
6. **Obligation type** — automatic vs consultative/discretionary, stated explicitly
7. **Historical invocations** — named, dated cases with documented outcomes
8. **Membership/jurisdictional scope** — who is bound; who is excluded or opted out
9. **Escalation/exit mechanics**
10. **Falsifier** — the primary-source fact that would disprove cell 4 or cell 6
11. **OPEN** — classified, disputed, or unresolved; not estimated
12. **Primary sources**

### UN (United Nations) Charter Chapter VII (Security Council authorization)

1. **Name:** UN Charter, **Chapter VII** — “Action with Respect to Threats to the Peace, Breaches of the Peace, and Acts of Aggression.” Common: “UN authorized the war,” mashed with Article 51.
2. **Type:** Legal authorization mechanism (collective security).
3. **Legal basis:** Charter Art. 39: the Security Council “shall determine the existence of any threat to the peace, breach of the peace, or act of aggression and shall make recommendations, or decide what measures shall be taken in accordance with Articles 41 and 42.” Art. 41: measures **not** involving armed force (interruption of economic relations, communications, diplomatic relations). Art. 42: if Art. 41 measures “would be inadequate or have proved to be inadequate,” the Council “may take such action by air, sea, or land forces as may be necessary.” Art. 25: Members “agree to accept and carry out the decisions of the Security Council.” Art. 2(4) is the background prohibition on the threat or use of force. https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/full-text
4. **Decision authority:** The **UN Security Council** acting under Art. 27: substantive decisions require nine affirmative votes including the **concurring votes of the five permanent members** (**P5**: China, France, Russian Federation, United Kingdom, United States) — the veto. Not the General Assembly (Uniting for Peace is a **separate**, contested machine — do not merge). Not the Secretary-General.
5. **Trigger:** Council **determination** under Art. 39 of a threat to the peace, breach of the peace, or act of aggression — not an “armed attack” test (that is Art. 51).
6. **Obligation type:** **Not automatic war.** Art. 42 *authorizes* the Council to take/authorize force; Art. 43 special agreements for standing UN forces were **never concluded**. Members must carry out **binding** Chapter VII *decisions* (Art. 25), including Art. 41 sanctions. Troop contribution to a Chapter VII *coalition* remains a **national constitutional** decision (**UK** (United Kingdom) Cabinet Manual 9.19: “Member States are not bound to deploy troops to peacekeeping missions”).
7. **Invocations (named, not a census):**
   - **Korea, June 1950:** Security Council resolution **S/RES/83** (UN document symbol: Security Council / Resolution; 27 June 1950) and S/RES/84 recommended assistance to the Republic of Korea — passed during the Soviet boycott. Outcome: US-led coalition under a UN Unified Command. **Do not** treat this as a template that survives a P5 veto.
   - **Iraq–Kuwait, 29 Nov 1990:** S/RES/678 authorized Member States cooperating with Kuwait to use “all necessary means” if Iraq did not comply by 15 Jan 1991. Outcome: Operation Desert Storm.
   - **Libya, 17 Mar 2011:** S/RES/1973 authorized a no-fly zone and “all necessary measures” to protect civilians, **excluding** a foreign occupation force. Outcome: NATO-led Operation Unified Protector; mandate-scope disputes **OPEN** as legal characterization, not as a casualty count.
   - **Kosovo 1999:** no Chapter VII force authorization. NATO Operation Allied Force proceeded on other claimed bases — **not** this stack.
8. **Scope:** All UN Members for Art. 25 decisions. P5 veto can block. Non-members are not Art. 25 addressees (Art. 2(6) is a separate, weaker clause).
9. **Exit:** A later Council resolution can terminate or modify the mandate. Measures under Art. 42 are to restore international peace and security. No “sixty-day clock” in the Charter.
10. **Falsifier:** If Art. 42 required every Member to send forces without a Council decision and without national constitutional processes, cell 6 dies. If Art. 39 vested the determination in the Secretary-General, cell 4 dies.
11. **OPEN:** Whether “all necessary means” in a given resolution authorizes regime change (Libya 2011 dispute). Uniting for Peace (General Assembly resolution **A/RES/377(V)**) as a substitute for a vetoed Chapter VII — **not resolved here**.
12. **Sources:** UN Charter Ch. VII and Arts. 2(4), 25, 27; S/RES/83, 84, 678, 1973.

### UN Charter Article 51 (inherent self-defence)

1. **Name:** Charter **Article 51**. Common: “UN permission to go to war,” mashed with Chapter VII.
2. **Type:** Legal declaration / inherent-right reservation.
3. **Legal basis:** Art. 51: “Nothing in the present Charter shall impair the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence **if an armed attack occurs** against a Member of the United Nations, **until** the Security Council has taken measures necessary to maintain international peace and security. Measures taken by Members… shall be immediately **reported** to the Security Council and shall not in any way affect the authority and responsibility of the Security Council…”
4. **Decision authority:** The **attacked Member’s own constitutional war-powers actor** (for the United States: the triangle in the next entry). Collective self-defence additionally requires a request/consent of the attacked state as a matter of customary law (*Nicaragua* **ICJ** (International Court of Justice) 1986 is the leading judgment — cite it as the Court, not as a Wikipedia gloss). The Security Council does **not** “grant” Art. 51; it receives the report and may take over.
5. **Trigger:** “**if an armed attack occurs**” against a Member. Anticipatory/pre-emptive use is **not** in the Charter text — **OPEN** as customary-law dispute (Caroline formula vs. Bush doctrine), not filled here.
6. **Obligation type:** **No obligation on third states to fight.** Art. 51 *preserves a right*. Collective self-defence is optional unless a separate treaty (NATO Art. 5, Japan Art. V) adds a further duty — and those treaties still leave the *means* to constitutional processes (see those entries). Reporting to the Council is mandatory; fighting for someone else is not.
7. **Invocations:**
   - **United States letter to the Security Council, 7 October 2001** (S/2001/946): Operation Enduring Freedom reported as Art. 51 self-defence after 11 September 2001. Parallel to, **not the same as**, NATO Art. 5.
   - **S/RES/1368** (12 Sept 2001) recognized the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence — a Council *recognition*, not an Art. 42 authorization of Afghanistan.
   - States’ Art. 51 letters after strikes on non-state armed groups in third countries: **OPEN** as a class (legality of “unwilling or unable” is disputed).
8. **Scope:** Members of the United Nations. Non-members: customary self-defence exists outside the Charter; Art. 51’s reporting clause is Charter-specific.
9. **Exit:** Right continues only “until” the Council has taken necessary measures. When that *until* is satisfied is **OPEN** in practice (Council rarely declares Art. 51 exhausted).
10. **Falsifier:** If Art. 51 required a prior Council authorization before a victim state could use force, it would be Chapter VII, and this split would die. If Art. 51 obligated all Members to join the victim, cell 6 dies.
11. **OPEN:** Armed-attack threshold for non-state actors; anticipatory self-defence; when Council “measures” terminate the right.
12. **Sources:** Charter Art. 51; S/2001/946; S/RES/1368 (2001); ICJ *Military and Paramilitary Activities in and against Nicaragua* (Merits), 1986 **I.C.J.** (International Court of Justice Reports) 14 (on collective self-defence request).

### NATO Article 5 (Washington Treaty)

1. **Name:** North Atlantic Treaty (Washington, **4 April 1949**), **Article 5**. Common: “NATO automatically goes to war.”
2. **Type:** Alliance treaty (collective defence), nested in Art. 51.
3. **Legal basis:** Art. 5: an armed attack against one or more Parties “in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all” and consequently each Party, in exercise of Art. 51 self-defence, “will assist the Party or Parties so attacked by taking forthwith, **individually and in concert** with the other Parties, **such action as it deems necessary, including the use of armed force**, to restore and maintain the security of the North Atlantic area.” Art. 4: consultation when territorial integrity, political independence or security “is threatened.” Art. 6: geographic/force definition of “armed attack.” Art. 11: provisions “carried out by the Parties in accordance with their respective **constitutional processes**.” https://www.nato.int/cps/en/natohq/official_texts_17120.htm
4. **Decision authority:** **Two layers.** (a) The **North Atlantic Council** (**NAC**, Art. 9) — Allies represented, **consensus**, no majority vote (UK Cabinet Manual 9.20) — decides whether Art. 5 is **invoked** as an Alliance political fact. (b) **Each Party’s own constitutional actor** decides what assistance “it deems necessary” (Art. 5 + Art. 11). For the US, that is still Congress/President under the war-powers triangle, not **SACEUR** (Supreme Allied Commander Europe).
5. **Trigger:** “Armed attack” as defined in Art. 6 (territory in Europe or North America, Turkey, islands north of the Tropic of Cancer; forces/vessels/aircraft in specified areas). **Not** an attack solely on a Partner nation, and **not** every cyber incident — whether a given cyber operation is an Art. 6 “armed attack” is **OPEN**.
6. **Obligation type:** **Consultative / individually discretionary as to means, including whether to use armed force.** The duty is to *assist* by action each Ally “deems necessary.” Armed force is listed as an *included option*, not a required output. NATO’s own 12 Sept 2001 NAC statement restates: “each Ally will assist the Party that has been attacked by taking such action as it deems necessary.” This is the opposite of automatic war.
7. **Invocations:**
   - **12 September 2001:** NAC agreed that if the attacks on the United States were directed from abroad, they would be covered by Art. 5. https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/2001/09/12/statement-by-the-north-atlantic-council
   - **2 October 2001:** Secretary General announced the foreign-direction determination; Art. 5 “fully operative.” Assistance included **AWACS** (Airborne Warning and Control System) aircraft over the US and naval patrols — **not** a single integrated invasion force under NAC command as the opening move. Operation Enduring Freedom remained **US-led** under Art. 51.
   - **Only invocation to date** (NATO topic page). Art. 4 has been invoked more often (e.g. Poland/Baltics after 2014/2022) — **consultation, not Art. 5**.
8. **Scope:** Current Allies as acceded under Art. 10 (unanimous invitation). Art. 6 excludes most of the globe (an attack on a US vessel in the South China Sea is **not** automatically Art. 5 geography). Former Algerian departments: inapplicable from **3 July 1962** (NAC note). France’s 1966 military-structure withdrawal was **not** denunciation of the Treaty.
9. **Exit:** Art. 13: after twenty years, any Party may cease to be a Party **one year** after notice of denunciation deposited with the US. Art. 5 measures terminate when the Security Council has taken necessary measures (Art. 5 ¶2).
10. **Falsifier:** If Art. 5 required each Ally to declare war or to place forces under SACEUR without a national “deems necessary” step, cell 6 dies. If the Supreme Allied Commander could invoke Art. 5, cell 4 dies.
11. **OPEN:** Cyber/hybrid threshold; Article 5 coverage of space; whether an attack on Guam (Pacific, south of the Art. 6 Atlantic/Europe box) is Art. 5 — **do not guess**.
12. **Sources:** North Atlantic Treaty Arts. 4–6, 9, 11, 13; NAC 12 Sept 2001 statement; NATO 2 Oct 2001 confirmation.

### US war-powers triangle (Art. I §8 / Art. II / War Powers Resolution)

1. **Name:** United States **declaration-of-war power**, **Commander-in-Chief power**, and **War Powers Resolution of 1973** (**WPR**; Public Law **Pub. L.** 93-148, 50 **U.S.C.** (United States Code) §§ 1541–1548). Common: “the President declares war.”
2. **Type:** Legal declaration mechanism (domestic).
3. **Legal basis:**
   - **U.S. Const.** (Constitution of the United States) **Art. I, §8, cl. 11:** Congress shall have power “To declare War, grant Letters of Marque and Reprisal, and make Rules concerning Captures on Land and Water.”
   - **Art. II, §2:** “The President shall be Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy of the United States, and of the Militia of the several States, when called into the actual Service of the United States.”
   - **50 U.S.C. § 1541(c):** the President’s Commander-in-Chief power to introduce forces into hostilities “are exercised only pursuant to (1) a declaration of war, (2) specific statutory authorization, or (3) a national emergency created by attack upon the United States, its territories or possessions, or its armed forces.”
   - **50 U.S.C. § 1543:** written report to Congress within **48 hours**. **§ 1544(b):** 60-day clock (extendable 30 days for withdrawal) unless Congress has declared war, enacted specific authorization, or is physically unable to meet.
4. **Decision authority:** **Congress** holds the declare-war power (Art. I). **The President** commands forces once in the field (Art. II) and claims independent defensive/initiating authority the executive branch does **not** concede is limited by § 1541(c) (**CRS** (Congressional Research Service) LSB11232: executive branch “contends that it is not legally bound by this interpretation”). Courts have largely **not** issued a final merits holding that the Resolution is unconstitutional or that it is fully enforceable — **OPEN** as litigation, not as a civics slogan.
5. **Trigger:** Formal **declaration of war** (joint resolution/bill signed by the President, historically). Or an **AUMF** (Authorization for Use of Military Force) as “specific statutory authorization.” Or an **armed attack** on the US/territories/forces under the Resolution’s third prong. Hostilities “or… imminent involvement in hostilities” for the reporting/clock provisions.
6. **Obligation type:** **No automatic war.** Congress *may* declare or authorize; it is not required to. The President is **not** obligated by NATO Art. 5 to introduce forces without a US constitutional step (Treaty Art. 11). The 60-day clock is Congress’s claimed *limit*, not an obligation to fight.
7. **Invocations:**
   - **Last formal declarations of war:** **5 June 1942**, against Bulgaria, Hungary, and Rumania (Romania), after presidential request 2 June 1942 (Senate historical table; 56 Stat. 307). https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/declarations-of-war.htm Korea, Vietnam, Gulf 1991, Afghanistan, Iraq 2002, Libya 2011 were **not** declarations of war.
   - **Pub. L. 107-40** (14 Sept 2001 AUMF); **Pub. L. 107-243** (16 Oct 2002 Iraq AUMF) — authorizations, not declarations.
   - Presidents have submitted **hundreds** of WPR reports; many operations continue under claimed Art. II or old AUMFs. **OPEN** a current count from the latest CRS compilation rather than inventing one.
8. **Scope:** US armed forces. Does not bind allies. Declarations of war historically named **specific enemy states**.
9. **Exit:** A later statute can repeal an AUMF. WPR § 1544 withdrawal clock (executive non-acquiescence **OPEN**). Peace treaties terminated the 1942 wars (e.g. 1947 peace treaties with Bulgaria/Hungary/Romania).
10. **Falsifier:** If Art. I §8 did not vest the declare-war power in Congress, cell 4 dies. If 50 U.S.C. § 1541(c) stated that NATO Art. 5 is itself a declaration of war, cell 6 dies.
11. **OPEN:** Constitutionality and justiciability of the War Powers Resolution; whether 2001 AUMF covers a named current group; **OLC** (Office of Legal Counsel, Department of Justice) opinions still classified.
12. **Sources:** U.S. Const. Art. I §8 cl. 11, Art. II §2; 50 U.S.C. §§ 1541, 1543–1544; Senate declarations table; CRS RL31133 / LSB11232.

### United Kingdom: Royal Prerogative and the parliamentary convention

1. **Name:** UK **Royal Prerogative** to deploy the Armed Forces, plus the **2011 Cabinet Manual convention** on Commons debate. Common: “Parliament declares war” / “the UK War Powers Act.”
2. **Type:** Legal declaration mechanism (prerogative + non-binding convention).
3. **Legal basis:** No War Powers Act. Cabinet Manual (1st ed., Oct 2011) **§3.34:** prerogative executive powers “include powers in relation to foreign affairs, to **deploy the Armed Forces**.” **§5.36–5.38:** practice of notifying the Commons; Iraq 2003 and Libya 2011 substantive debates; **§5.38:** “In 2011, the Government acknowledged that a convention had developed in Parliament that before troops were committed the House of Commons should have an opportunity to debate the matter and said that it proposed to observe that convention except when there was an emergency and such action would not be appropriate.” https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/60641/cabinet-manual.pdf NATO: Manual **§9.20:** Allies “maintain the freedom to decide **how** they will respond.”
4. **Decision authority:** **Ministers**, in practice the **Prime Minister** with **Cabinet**, exercising the prerogative **on behalf of the Crown**. The Sovereign does not personally order deployments. The House of Commons, under the convention, is offered a **debate** (not a statutory veto).
5. **Trigger:** A government decision to commit forces to armed conflict (convention) or any deployment the ministers choose (prerogative). Emergency exception in §5.38.
6. **Obligation type:** **Discretionary as to war; convention is not legally binding.** Commons Library **CBP** (Commons Briefing Paper) 10001: “parliament still has no legal role in approving the deployment of the armed forces.” The 2003 Iraq vote did not convert the prerogative into statute.
7. **Invocations:**
   - **18 March 2003:** Commons voted to support the use of all means necessary to ensure Iraqi disarmament — political authorization, not a legal condition precedent.
   - **21 March 2011:** Libya motion after **PM** (Prime Minister) statement 18 March (Manual §5.37).
   - **29 August 2013:** Commons **rejected** the government’s Syria motion. The government did not proceed with that strike package — convention **observed as politics**, still not a statute. (Commons Library CBP-7166).
8. **Scope:** UK Armed Forces. Devolved administrations do not hold the war prerogative.
9. **Exit:** Ministers can halt operations. No treaty-style denunciation of the prerogative. A future statute could abolish or condition it (OPEN if enacted).
10. **Falsifier:** If a UK statute required prior Commons approval as a condition of lawful deployment, cell 6’s “not legally binding” clause would need a rewrite. If the Manual vested the deployment power in the Commons Speaker, cell 4 dies.
11. **OPEN:** Whether 2015 Syria/Daesh votes, 2018 Syria chemical-weapons strikes without a prior vote, and any 2024–26 deployments have amended the convention by practice.
12. **Sources:** Cabinet Manual 2011 §§3.34, 5.36–5.38, 9.19–9.20; Commons Library CBP-10001, CBP-7166.

### US–Japan Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security, Article V

1. **Name:** Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security between Japan and the United States of America, Washington, **19 January 1960**, **Article V**. Common: “NATO for Japan.”
2. **Type:** Alliance treaty (bilateral).
3. **Legal basis:** Art. V: “Each Party recognizes that an armed attack against either Party **in the territories under the administration of Japan** would be dangerous to its own peace and safety and declares that it would **act to meet the common danger in accordance with its constitutional provisions and processes**.” Report to the UN Security Council under Art. 51; terminate when the Council has taken necessary measures. Art. IV: consultation. Art. X: after ten years, either Party may terminate with **one year’s** notice. **MOFA** (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan) authentic English: https://www.mofa.go.jp/region/n-america/us/q&a/ref/1.html
4. **Decision authority:** **Each Party’s constitutional processes** — for the US, the war-powers triangle; for Japan, the Cabinet under the Constitution of Japan (Art. 9 / 2015 security legislation as the domestic implementation machine — **do not** merge 1960 Art. V with 2015 legislation as one clause). No NATO-style North Atlantic Council.
5. **Trigger:** “Armed attack against either Party **in the territories under the administration of Japan**.” An armed attack on **Hawaii or California is outside Art. V’s geographic sentence**. Contrast NATO Art. 5 (Europe or North America) and **ANZUS** (Security Treaty between Australia, New Zealand, and the United States of America) Art. IV (Pacific Area).
6. **Obligation type:** **Not automatic war; not NATO Art. 5’s “attack against them all.”** Duty is to “act to meet the common danger” **as each constitution provides**. No “deems necessary including armed force” clause — weaker on means than NATO, narrower on geography.
7. **Invocations:** No public invocation of Art. V analogous to NATO 12 Sept 2001. The Treaty has structured US forces in Japan (Art. VI / **SOFA** (Status of Forces Agreement)) through crises (Taiwan Strait, Korean peninsula contingencies) **without** a formal Art. V trigger announcement — **OPEN** whether any classified consultation counts as “invocation.”
8. **Scope:** Two Parties. Senkaku/Diaoyu administration: US statements that Art. V applies because Japan administers the islands are **political notes verbales**, not a treaty amendment — name them as policy, not as a third party’s consent.
9. **Exit:** Art. X one-year notice. Art. V measures yield to Security Council action.
10. **Falsifier:** If Art. V stated that an attack on the US homeland is an attack on Japan requiring **SDF** (Self-Defense Forces) deployment without Diet/Cabinet processes, cell 5/6 die. If a NATO-style council could order US forces to Japan, cell 4 dies.
11. **OPEN:** Application to a Taiwan contingency that is not an armed attack in Japanese-administered territory; 2015 legislation’s fit with Art. 9.
12. **Sources:** 1960 Treaty Arts. IV, V, VI, X (MOFA).

### ANZUS and the 1986 New Zealand rupture

1. **Name:** Security Treaty between Australia, New Zealand, and the United States of America (**ANZUS**), San Francisco, **1 September 1951**, in force **29 April 1952**. Common: “the Pacific NATO.”
2. **Type:** Alliance treaty (trilateral on paper; functionally fractured).
3. **Legal basis:** Art. III: Parties “will consult together whenever in the opinion of any of them the territorial integrity, political independence or security of any of the Parties is threatened in the Pacific.” Art. IV: each Party “recognizes that an armed attack in the **Pacific Area** on any of the Parties would be dangerous to its own peace and safety and declares that it would **act to meet the common danger in accordance with its constitutional processes**.” (**ATS** (Australian Treaty Series) 1952 No. 2; **UNTS** (United Nations Treaty Series) 131 p. 83; **TIAS** (Treaties and Other International Acts Series) 2493.) Depositary note: US announced **11 August 1986** “the suspension of its obligations under the Treaty in respect of New Zealand”; Australia–US exchange of letters the same day reaffirmed commitments between them. https://www.austlii.edu.au/au/other/dfat/treaty_list/depository/anzus.html
4. **Decision authority:** Each Party’s **constitutional processes** (Art. IV). No NAC. After 1986, **US–NZ** (New Zealand) Art. IV obligations are treated by Washington as suspended; US–Australia continues. US Embassy Wellington: NZ nuclear-ship ban “effectively prevented practical alliance cooperation”; “In 1986 the United States suspended its ANZUS security obligations to New Zealand.” https://nz.usembassy.gov/policy-and-history/
5. **Trigger:** Armed attack in the Pacific Area on any Party (Art. IV); threat in the Pacific for consultation (Art. III).
6. **Obligation type:** **Consultative + discretionary action under constitutional processes — not automatic.** Same family of wording as US–Japan Art. V, **not** NATO Art. 5. After 1986 the US–NZ security obligation is **the documented lapse**.
7. **Invocations:**
   - **Australia, 14 September 2001:** Prime Minister Howard announced Australia would invoke ANZUS in response to the attacks on the US — a **national political invocation**, not a trilateral council minute. Outcome: Australian forces in Afghanistan; **not** a US declaration of war.
   - **11 August 1986:** US suspension vis-à-vis New Zealand after NZ’s nuclear-free port policy collided with US neither-confirm-nor-deny. Treaty **not formally terminated**. Australia–US reaffirmation same day (**AFAR** (Australian Foreign Affairs Record) 1986 p. 739 per **DFAT** (Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade, Australia)).
8. **Scope:** Originally three. UK excluded (not a party). NZ still a signatory; US security guarantee to NZ **suspended** as a matter of US position. Wellington Declaration 2010 and later cooperation are **not** a silent un-suspension of Art. IV unless a primary instrument so states — **OPEN**.
9. **Exit:** Treaty silent on “suspension”; the US did not plead **VCLT** (Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties) Arts. 60/62 in the DFAT depositary note. Art. X: “This Treaty shall remain in force indefinitely. Any Party may cease to be a member of the Council established by Article VII one year after notice of denunciation…” — **Council** exit is specified; wholesale treaty death is **not** as cleanly drafted as NATO Art. 13. OPEN as a drafting gap the 1986 suspension exploited.
10. **Falsifier:** If the 11 Aug 1986 US announcement did not suspend US obligations toward New Zealand, cell 6/7 die. If Art. IV required automatic armed force, it would be NATO Art. 5 language, which it is not.
11. **OPEN:** Present legal status of US–NZ Art. IV (suspended vs. revived vs. replaced by political declarations); Australia’s 1986 legal characterization.
12. **Sources:** ANZUS Arts. III, IV, X (UNTS/TIAS/ATS); DFAT depositary page; US Embassy Wellington policy page.

### Proxy pattern: US covert aid to Afghan insurgents, 1979–89 (no war-declaration stack)

1. **Name:** US **covert action** in Afghanistan, 1979–89, channeled in part through Pakistan’s **ISI** (Inter-Services Intelligence). Popular label “Operation Cyclone” is a later/operational nickname — use the **finding** as the legal instrument.
2. **Type:** Proxy-conflict pattern (no treaty trigger; no declaration of war).
3. **Legal basis:** Not UN Art. 42, not a declaration of war, not NATO. Domestic: **presidential finding** and congressional notice under the **Hughes–Ryan** framework then in force; now codified at **50 U.S.C. § 3093** (written finding that covert action is necessary to support identifiable foreign-policy objectives and important to national security; reported to the intelligence committees). Covert action = activity to influence conditions abroad where the US role “will not be apparent or acknowledged publicly,” excluding traditional military/diplomatic activity (§ 3093(e)). **FRUS** (Foreign Relations of the United States) 1977–80 vol. XII: **3 July 1979** findings — cash/non-lethal supplies and propaganda, **up to $695,000** on the non-military finding; **7 November 1979** further finding; **28 December 1979** lethal-weapons finding after the Soviet intervention. https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v12/d267
4. **Decision authority:** The **President of the United States** (finding). **CIA** (Central Intelligence Agency) executes. Congress’s intelligence committees receive notice; this is **not** an AUMF and **not** a declaration of war. Pakistani **ISI** is a foreign service, not a US constitutional actor — a **channel**, not a co-author of the finding.
5. **Trigger:** None in the law of armed conflict of a declared international armed conflict between the US and the **USSR** (Union of Soviet Socialist Republics) / **DRA** (Democratic Republic of Afghanistan). The statutory trigger is the President’s determination under the finding statute. Soviet 24–27 December 1979 intervention dated the **lethal** expansion, not the July non-lethal start.
6. **Obligation type:** **No alliance obligation to the mujahideen and no automatic US combat.** The finding *authorizes* US agencies; it does **not** obligate Pakistan, Saudi matching funds (FRUS notes Saudi matching as later authorization — amounts often **[not declassified]**), or US ground combat.
7. **Invocations / outcomes:** Program ran through the 1980s (Reagan-era expansions; **NSDD** (National Security Decision Directive)-level documents exist — **OPEN** which remain classified). Geneva Accords **14 April 1988**; Soviet withdrawal completed **15 February 1989**. No US–USSR declaration of war. Troop/spending totals differ by CRS, CIA histories, and Pakistani accounts — **do not mash**.
8. **Scope:** US covert-action statute binds US agencies. It does not create rights in Afghan commanders. Stingers and other lethal aid: **OPEN** exact delivery dates from declassified tables this pass.
9. **Exit:** President can rescind a finding; Congress can restrict via intelligence authorization/appropriations riders. No Art. 13 denunciation because there is no treaty.
10. **Falsifier:** If the 3 July 1979 instruments were declarations of war or Chapter VII resolutions, this would not be the “no legal trigger” category. If § 3093 made ISI a US executive officer, cell 4 dies.
11. **OPEN:** Classified annexes and dollar totals after 1979; degree of US knowledge of downstream diversion; whether any still-classified finding purported to authorize US combat.
12. **Sources:** 50 U.S.C. § 3093; FRUS 1977–80 XII Docs. 59, 76, 267; Hughes–Ryan (historical) as the then-notice statute.

### Nuclear deterrence: NPT (Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons) plus US 2022 Nuclear Posture Review

1. **Name:** Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (**NPT**, 1 July 1968, in force 5 March 1970) **plus** United States **2022 Nuclear Posture Review** (**NPR**; unclassified, **DoD** (Department of Defense), with **NDS** (National Defense Strategy)). Common: “NPT bans first use” / “US has a no-first-use treaty.”
2. **Type:** Deterrence doctrine sitting on a non-proliferation treaty — **two machines**.
3. **Legal basis:**
   - NPT Art. I (nuclear-weapon States not to transfer nuclear weapons or assist non-nuclear-weapon States to manufacture them); Art. II (**NNWS** (non-nuclear-weapon States) not to receive/manufacture); Art. VI (pursue negotiations on cessation of the arms race and nuclear disarmament). The NPT **does not** contain a no-first-use clause. **IAEA** (International Atomic Energy Agency) **INFCIRC** (Information Circular) 140.
   - **2022 NPR** (DoD unclassified, integrated with the 2022 National Defense Strategy): “The United States would only consider the use of nuclear weapons in **extreme circumstances** to defend the vital interests of the United States or its Allies and partners.” Negative security assurance: will not use or threaten nuclear weapons against NNWS party to the NPT **and in compliance** with non-proliferation obligations. Review “concluded that [No First Use and Sole Purpose] would result in an unacceptable level of risk.” https://media.defense.gov/2022/Oct/27/2003103845/-1/-1/1/2022-NATIONAL-DEFENSE-STRATEGY-NPR-MDR.pdf
4. **Decision authority:** **Employment:** the **President of the United States** (US nuclear command-and-control). NPR is DoD/White House declaratory policy, **not** a Senate-ratified protocol. NPT obligations: States Parties. First use against a complying NNWS would break the **declaratory** **NSA** (negative security assurance), not a separate NPT article.
5. **Trigger:** No treaty trigger for launch. NPR: “extreme circumstances” / “vital interests” — **deliberately non-numeric**. Adversary nuclear or certain non-nuclear strategic attacks are discussed as contingencies; that is doctrine, not a statute.
6. **Obligation type:** **NPT: non-transfer / non-acquisition, not a war obligation. NPR: discretionary, not no-first-use.** The US **rejected** **NFU** (no-first-use) and sole purpose in 2022. China-style NFU is **another state’s** doctrine (subdivisions). NATO nuclear sharing is a **third** machine (OPEN as to NPT Art. I debate).
7. **Invocations:** Nuclear weapons have not been used in conflict since 1945. Declaratory documents: 2010 NPR NSA language; 2022 NPR reaffirmation/rejection of NFU. Extended deterrence commitments to NATO, Japan, **ROK** (Republic of Korea) are policy, not NPT articles.
8. **Scope:** NPT nearly universal; non-parties (India, Pakistan, Israel; **DPRK** (Democratic People’s Republic of Korea) withdrawal claimed) are **outside** Art. II. US NSA **excludes** NNWS not in compliance and all nuclear-armed states.
9. **Exit:** NPT Art. X: withdrawal with three months’ notice if extraordinary events jeopardize supreme interests. NPR can be rewritten by a later administration without Senate advice and consent.
10. **Falsifier:** If the 2022 NPR adopted a no-first-use pledge, cell 6 dies. If NPT Art. VI were a first-use ban, cell 3 dies.
11. **OPEN:** Classified annexes of the NPR; exact presidential launch authorities and timing; whether a given NNWS is “in compliance.”
12. **Sources:** NPT Arts. I, II, VI, X (IAEA INFCIRC/140); 2022 NPR/NDS PDF (DoD).

### Private military/security contractors (API (Additional Protocol I) Art. 47 gray zone)

1. **Name:** Private military/security contractors (**PMSCs**) under international humanitarian law, especially Additional Protocol I (1977) Article 47 (mercenaries) and US **MEJA** (Military Extraterritorial Jurisdiction Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3261).
2. **Type:** Hybrid-warfare / status instrument (who is a combatant).
3. **Legal basis:** API Art. 47 (8 June 1977): “A mercenary shall not have the right to be a combatant or a prisoner of war.” A mercenary is any person who **meets all of** (a)–(f): specially recruited to fight; does take a direct part in hostilities; motivated essentially by private gain **and** promised pay **substantially in excess** of that of comparable armed-forces members; not a national/resident of a Party; not a member of a Party’s armed forces; not sent on official duty by a non-Party State. https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/article-47 Geneva Convention III Art. 4 remains the **POW** (prisoner of war) test for those who **are** members of armed forces or accompanying forces with identity documents (**ID**). The **United States is not a party to API**; it treats much of API as customary in part — **OPEN** which of Art. 47’s six limbs the US accepts as custom. MEJA: US criminal jurisdiction over certain contractors supporting DoD overseas.
4. **Decision authority:** **Status** is not “declared” by a PMSC firm. **Combatant status** is determined by **IHL** (international humanitarian law) criteria and, in US operations, by the **combatant commander / DoD** rules for use of force and contractor clauses (**DFARS** (Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement)), plus **host-nation** criminal jurisdiction under a SOFA. Hiring a contractor is a **contracting officer / agency** decision, not an Art. I declaration of war.
5. **Trigger:** None for “starting a war.” Direct participation in hostilities can make an individual a lawful target for the duration of that participation (**ICRC** (International Committee of the Red Cross) **DPH** (direct participation in hostilities) guidance is **guidance**, not a treaty — cite as such).
6. **Obligation type:** **No state obligation to treat a contractor as a mercenary unless the six-part test is met.** Most armed-forces contractors fail (e)–(f) or (c)’s “substantially in excess” / “fight” limbs. **No automatic combatant privilege.** Failure of Art. 47 does not equal POW status.
7. **Invocations / cases:**
   - **Nisour Square, Baghdad, 16 September 2007** (Blackwater): US criminal prosecutions under MEJA — contractors treated as **criminal defendants**, not POWs, not Art. 47 mercenaries as the charging theory. Outcomes later affected by pardons — **name the docket, don’t collapse into a morality play**.
   - **US not party to API** — Art. 47 was not the charging statute.
8. **Scope:** API binds States Parties in international armed conflict. Non-international armed conflict: **AP II** (Additional Protocol II) has **no** mercenary article. UN Mercenary Convention 1989 is **another** treaty with a different definition — US not a party; do not mash with Art. 47.
9. **Exit:** Contract termination; MEJA prosecution; host-nation prosecution if SOFA allows. No “denunciation of mercenary status.”
10. **Falsifier:** If API Art. 47 made every armed contractor a mercenary without the six-part test, cell 6 dies. If MEJA were a declaration of war, cell 2 dies.
11. **OPEN:** US customary-law position on Art. 47; Wagner-type forces’ status when integrated into a state’s armed forces; classified contractor **ROE** (rules of engagement).
12. **Sources:** API Art. 47 (ICRC); **GC III** (Geneva Convention III) Art. 4; 18 U.S.C. § 3261; DoD Law of War Manual (public edition) on contractors/civilians accompanying the force.

### Economic sanctions: IEEPA (International Emergency Economic Powers Act) and UN Chapter VII Article 41

1. **Name:** United States **International Emergency Economic Powers Act** (**IEEPA**, Pub. L. 95-223, **28 Dec 1977**, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1708) **and**, separately, UN Charter **Article 41** sanctions. Common: “sanctions equal a declaration of war.”
2. **Type:** Hybrid / non-kinetic coercive instrument.
3. **Legal basis:**
   - **50 U.S.C. § 1701(a):** IEEPA authorities “may be exercised to deal with any unusual and extraordinary threat, which has its source in whole or substantial part outside the United States, to the national security, foreign policy, or economy of the United States, **if the President declares a national emergency** with respect to such threat.” § 1702: regulate transactions, freeze assets, etc. Nested under the **National Emergencies Act** (**NEA**, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1601 et seq.).
   - Charter **Art. 41:** Council may decide measures **not involving armed force**, including “complete or partial interruption of economic relations.” Binding via Art. 25.
4. **Decision authority:** **IEEPA:** the **President**, by proclamation of a national emergency (usually an **EO** (Executive Order) implemented by **OFAC** — Office of Foreign Assets Control, Department of the Treasury). Congress can terminate an emergency only by **joint resolution** after *INS v. Chadha* (**INS**: Immigration and Naturalization Service) killed the old concurrent-resolution legislative veto (NEA was amended accordingly — CRS R45618). **Art. 41:** **Security Council** (P5 veto applies), not OFAC.
5. **Trigger:** IEEPA: presidential finding of an unusual and extraordinary **foreign-sourced** threat **plus** NEA emergency declaration. Art. 41: Art. 39 determination + Council decision. **Not** “armed attack.”
6. **Obligation type:** **IEEPA: discretionary presidential power, not a duty to sanction and not a duty to use force.** Art. 41: Members must carry out **Council decisions**; they are **not** thereby authorized to use Art. 42 force. Do not treat an EO under IEEPA as implementing Art. 41 unless the EO says it implements a named resolution (sometimes it does — name the resolution).
7. **Invocations:** Hundreds of IEEPA emergencies (Iran 1979 hostage crisis as the early modern template; many successors). UN comprehensive sanctions on Iraq (S/RES/661, 1990) then 678 force authorization — **two sequential Chapter VII tools**, not one. CRS R45618 on IEEPA’s later uses (including 2025 tariff litigation) — **cite the statute first**; court outcomes **OPEN** if still pending at pull.
8. **Scope:** IEEPA binds US persons (and sometimes foreign persons via US-nexus). Art. 41 binds UN Members. **EU** (European Union) blocking statutes can **conflict** — another machine.
9. **Exit:** President terminates the emergency or Congress jointly so resolves; annual NEA renewal practice is often **pro forma** (CRS). Council resolution lifts Art. 41.
10. **Falsifier:** If § 1701 required a declaration of war before an asset freeze, cell 6 dies. If Art. 41 were the same as Art. 42, the kinetic/non-kinetic split dies.
11. **OPEN:** Pending Supreme Court/congressional fights over IEEPA’s outer bound (tariffs vs. traditional blocking); how many NEA emergencies are live this pull (count from the Federal Register, don’t guess).
12. **Sources:** 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1702; NEA 50 U.S.C. §§ 1601 et seq.; Charter Arts. 25, 39, 41, 42; CRS R45618.
